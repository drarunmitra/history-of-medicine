# 1. At a glance

**Aim.** Show students that public health began when people counted, mapped, compared and then acted on whole populations, often before they knew the cause.

# 5. Slide-by-slide notes

## Block A: Opening (slides 2 to 4, 3 minutes)

**Say.**

- Lecture 2 ended with dissection, experiment, the microscope and counting (Graunt's Bills of Mortality).
- Today those methods are used on whole populations in crowded industrial cities of the 1800s.
- "This is where public health begins: count, compare, then act, sometimes before the cause is known."
- Read the four objectives aloud. Point out the coloured phase on the timeline: about 1700 to the present.

**Ask.** "What did Graunt's tables tell us that one doctor looking at one patient could not?" Expected: patterns across a population.

**Show/Do.** Slide 4 is the L3 timeline. Slide 2 shows the full timeline. Do not linger.

**Watch out.** Do not read the objectives slowly. Students tune out.

## Block B: Hook and industrial revolution (slides 5 to 7, 5 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 5 is a Doré engraving of London, 1872. Give students 60 seconds to answer the question (section 6).
- Slide 6 is the industrial revolution diagram, 1750 to 1900. People moved to cities for work.
- Slide 7: crowding, no sewers, shared wells. Cholera, typhoid and tuberculosis followed.

**Ask.** "Which city problem is the cause and which is the effect?" Accept any sensible answer. Steer toward crowding plus no sewers plus shared water.

**Show/Do.** Slide 7 shows two cartoons: Punch, "A Court for King Cholera" (1852) and Cruikshank, "London going out of town" (1829). Ask what each one is saying.

**Watch out.** The slide says the image sources are "to be confirmed". Do not tell students the images are certain originals.

## Block C: Chadwick (slides 8 to 12, 7 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 8 table (average age at death, 1842): Rutland gentry 52, labourers 38. Liverpool gentry 35, labourers 15. Manchester gentry 38, labourers 17.
- The surprise: the Rutland labourer (38) outlived the Liverpool gentleman (35). "Where you lived mattered as much as who you were."
- The warning: this is average age at death, not life expectancy. It depends on how many young people live in the town. Farr said so almost at once.
- Slide 9: Chadwick was a Poor Law official. His inquiry began in 1839. The report came in July 1842 and was published at his own expense.
- Slide 10: constant water supply to every house; water-closets draining into sewers; waste carried away. He blamed foul air (miasma). The theory was wrong but the action was right.
- Slide 11: Public Health Act, royal assent 31 August 1848. It created a General Board of Health. It was permissive. The Board was abolished in 1858 and Chadwick was pushed out in 1854.

**Ask.** Slide 8: "Which is worse: a Liverpool gentleman or a Rutland labourer?" Expected: the Liverpool gentleman, because 35 is below 38. Then ask why.

**Show/Do.** On slide 8, click once to reveal the callout. Let students answer before you click.

**Watch out.**

- Do not call the figures "life expectancy". The slide itself warns against this.
- The slide notes the Manchester labourer figure is 17 in one table and 15 in another. Use 17 as on the slide.
- The Act was "permissive". Do not say it forced every town to act.

## Block D: Farr (slides 13 to 15, 4 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 13: Farr was Compiler of Abstracts at the General Register Office from 1839 or 1840. He built the national death registration system and a disease classification. He ran the cholera numbers for 1848 to 1849 (published 1852).
- Slide 14: in 38 London districts, cholera deaths fell as height above the Thames rose. That fitted miasma. Later reanalysis found water supply was also strongly linked, perhaps more than elevation. "The data were right. The explanation was wrong."
- Slide 15: in the 1850s Farr was the recognised authority and Snow the outsider. After the 1866 East London outbreak, which Farr traced to the Old Ford reservoir, he accepted that water carried cholera.

**Ask.** "Farr's data were correct. Why did they point to the wrong cause?" Expected: low ground also had the dirtiest water.

**Show/Do.** Slide 14 is the key slide. Slide 13 can take 30 seconds.

**Watch out.** Do not say Farr was "wrong". His data were right. His explanation was incomplete. The date "1839 or 1840" is split in the sources.

## Block E: Snow (slides 16 to 23, 9 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 16: Snow was a London physician and anaesthetist. In 1849 he argued that cholera spreads by water, not air. He had his hypothesis before 1854.
- Slide 17: the outbreak began in the night of 31 August to 1 September 1854. More than 500 fatal attacks in ten days. He marked deaths on a map and found them clustered around the Broad Street pump.
- Slide 18: two odd-one-out groups. The Poland Street workhouse: only 5 of 535 inmates died. It had its own well. The Broad Street brewery: about 70 workmen, none seriously ill. They drank malt liquor and had a deep well on site.
- Slide 19: the Hampstead widow. She lived away from Broad Street but liked its water. A cart brought a large bottle on 31 August. She died on 2 September. A niece who drank some also died. Snow called this "perhaps the most conclusive of all".
- Slide 20: the "grand experiment". Two water companies served the same streets. First seven weeks of 1854: Southwark & Vauxhall 315 deaths per 10,000 houses; Lambeth 37; rest of London 59.
- Slide 21: the pump handle, removed 8 September. Attacks were already falling.
- Slide 22: Rev. Henry Whitehead traced the likely first case to Frances Lewis, an infant at 40 Broad Street.
- Slide 23: the hypothesis came first and the map came later. "A map shows. Comparison proves."

**Ask.**

- After slide 18: "Why does the workhouse matter?" Expected: it was surrounded by deaths but had its own well. The air was the same. The water was different.
- After slide 20: "Why is this called an experiment?" Expected: two groups, same area, different exposure, no choice by the people.

**Show/Do.** Run the optional exercise (section 7) after slide 16 and before slide 17. For slide 21, click once to reveal the callout. Let students interpret the table first.

**Watch out.**

- Use one set of numbers per slide. Slide 20 uses the seven-week figures (315 vs 37). The first four weeks give 71 vs 5, which is 14 times. If you say "14 times", say the period.
- Slide 21 is the most common myth. Section 9 covers it.
- "Father of epidemiology" is contested. Use the slide's wording: "a founder of modern epidemiological method".
- The sources for Whitehead and Lewis are secondary. The date of the link differs. Say "by spring 1855".

## Block F: Shattuck (slide 24, 2 minutes)

**Say.**

- *Report of the Sanitary Commission of Massachusetts*, 1850.
- About 50 recommendations: state and local health boards, vital statistics, school health, nurse training.
- Massachusetts created a State Board of Health only in 1869.

**Ask.** "Why did the Board come nearly 20 years after the report?" Expected: politics and cost. Link back to slide 12.

**Watch out.** "About 50" is from secondary summaries. Keep the "about".

## Block G: Germ theory and four more people (slides 25 to 29, 6 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 25: 1854, Pacini described the comma-shaped cholera bacillus in Florence. 1883 to 1884, Koch isolated it in pure culture (Egypt, then Calcutta). Snow and Chadwick acted before the cause was proven. The microbes explained why it worked.
- Slide 26: Semmelweis, Vienna, 1847. Doctors and students lost more women to childbed fever than midwives did (roughly 10% against 4%). Doctors also did autopsies. He ordered handwashing in chlorinated lime in May 1847. Deaths fell sharply. He published in 1861 and was rejected.
- Slide 27: Lister, Glasgow. Carbolic acid on wounds, dressings and instruments. First case 1865, Lancet 1867. Pasteur gave the mechanism and Lister the practice.
- Slide 28: Nightingale, Crimean War, Scutari. About 42% in February 1855, about 2% by June, after a Sanitary Commission fixed drains and ventilation. Polar-area diagram, 1858.
- Slide 29: Ross, 20 August 1897, Secunderabad. Malaria parasites in a mosquito. Nobel Prize 1902. First mathematical model of malaria, 1911.

**Ask.** After slide 25: "Why does it matter that Snow and Chadwick acted before the germ was found?" Expected: action does not have to wait for full theory.

**Show/Do.** Say that the common thread is practice and data moved ahead of theory, then theory caught up. The Ross slide is local: Secunderabad is in Hyderabad.

**Watch out.**

- Semmelweis percentages are [verify]. Say "roughly".
- Nightingale: the slide credits the fall to the Sanitary Commission fixing drains and ventilation. Do not tell students nursing alone caused it. The slide says the figures are her own and debated.
- The Ross slide carries a "[verify citation]" note. Fix it before you share the deck.

## Block H: Smallpox, Bhore, Alma-Ata and recap (slides 30 to 32, 3 minutes)

**Say.**

- Slide 30: Jenner, 1796, 8-year-old James Phipps. WHO Intensified Programme began 1967. India's last case: May 1975, Assam. India certified free April 1977. WHO declared eradication in 1980.
- Slide 31: Bhore Committee (set up 1943, reported 1946): integrate preventive and curative care; build primary health centres. Alma-Ata Declaration, 12 September 1978: "Health for All" by 2000 through primary health care. 134 countries.
- Slide 32: read the five recap lines, one breath each.

**Ask.** "What is the one method that links Snow, Farr, Nightingale and the smallpox campaign?" Expected: collect data, compare, act.

**Watch out.** Do not quote Bhore's population norms for primary health centres. Sources conflict. Do not say "Bhore invented rural health". The slide says rural schemes existed before.

# 7. Optional activity (5 minutes): find the pump

**All data in this exercise are invented for teaching. They are not Snow's data.**

**Aim.** Let students do what Snow did: map deaths, find the pattern, then find the exception that points to water.

**Handout.** A small grid, columns A to E and rows 1 to 5. Three pumps. Ten households.

Pumps: Pump 1 at B2. Pump 2 at D2. Pump 3 at C5.

| Household | Grid square | Pump the household drinks from | Cholera deaths |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | D1 | Pump 2 | 3 |
| H2 | D3 | Pump 2 | 2 |
| H3 | E2 | Pump 2 | 2 |
| H4 | C2 | Pump 2 | 1 |
| H5 | B1 | Pump 1 | 0 |
| H6 | A3 | Pump 1 | 0 |
| H7 | B4 | Pump 1 | 0 |
| H8 | C5 | Pump 3 | 0 |
| H9 | D4 | Pump 3 | 0 |
| H10 | A5 | Pump 2 (sends a cart for it) | 2 |

**Instructions.**

1. Hand out the sheet. Students work in pairs for 3 minutes.
2. Task 1: "Mark each household on the grid. Write the number of deaths. Which pump is closest to the deaths?" (Expected: Pump 2.)
3. Task 2: "Two households do not fit the 'nearest pump' idea. Which ones? What do they tell you?" (Expected: H9 is close to Pump 2 but drinks from Pump 3 and has no deaths. H10 is far from Pump 2 but drinks its water and has deaths. So the cause is the water, not the air of the neighbourhood.)
4. Task 3: "What would you do on day one?" (Expected: close or lock Pump 2 and tell people to use another source.)

**What to do with the output.** Ask two pairs to say their answers. Write "H9" and "H10" on the board. Say: "These are your workhouse and your widow." Then show slide 17 and move on. Link it to slide 23: "A map shows. Comparison proves."

**Where it fits.** After slide 16, before slide 17. To make time, use the cut list in section 10 (cut Shattuck and shorten Farr).

# 8. Quiz

**How to run it (6 to 8 minutes).**

- Show one question at a time (slides 33 to 40). Allow 30 seconds each.
- Hands up: ask for a show of hands for a, b, c and d. Count roughly.
- Phone poll: use any free poll tool. Paste the question and options in advance.
- Before you click to the answers (slide 41), ask one student to defend an answer. Then reveal.
- If more than a third pick a wrong option on a question, spend 30 seconds on it.

For every question, the correct answer is in bold.

**Q1 (slide 33). Chadwick's 1842 report mainly argued for:**

- a) vaccination. Wrong. Chadwick's report was about water, drains and waste. Jenner's smallpox vaccine (slide 30) was a separate story.
- **b) sanitary reform (water, drains, waste). Correct.** Slide 10 lists exactly this.
- c) hospital building. Wrong. The sanitary idea was about the environment, not hospitals.
- d) quarantine. Wrong. His answer was clean water and drains, not isolation.

**Q2 (slide 34). William Farr is best known for:**

- a) the pump handle. Wrong. That is Snow's story, and its effect is disputed (slide 21).
- **b) systematic collection and analysis of vital statistics. Correct.** He built the death registration system and a disease classification (slide 13).
- c) germ theory. Wrong. Farr first favoured the miasma explanation (slide 14).
- d) the microscope. Wrong. Leeuwenhoek, from Lecture 2.

**Q3 (slide 35). Snow's key evidence in 1854 was:**

- a) the smell of the air. Wrong. That is the miasma view that Snow argued against.
- **b) cases clustered around one pump, plus a comparison of water supplies. Correct.** Slides 17 to 20.
- c) laboratory culture of the cholera bacillus. Wrong. Koch isolated it in 1883 to 1884, after Snow.
- d) autopsy findings. Wrong. The deck presents no autopsy evidence. Snow's case rested on the map and the comparisons.

**Q4 (slide 36). Chadwick and Snow both acted before the cause was proven. This shows that:**

- a) theory is useless. Wrong. Germ theory later explained why the actions worked (slide 25).
- **b) effective action can come from observing patterns. Correct.**
- c) miasma was correct. Wrong. Slide 10 says "wrong theory, but right action".
- d) sanitation did not help. Wrong. Clean water and drains worked.

**Q5 (slide 37). Shattuck's 1850 report recommended:**

- a) a war on tuberculosis. Wrong. It is not among the recommendations on slide 24.
- **b) local health boards and vital statistics. Correct.**
- c) compulsory vaccination. Wrong. Not listed on slide 24.
- d) a national hospital service. Wrong. His blueprint was state and local health boards.

**Q6 (slide 38). Which later work identified the cause of cholera in pure culture?**

- a) Hippocrates' humours. Wrong. Ancient theory (Lecture 1).
- b) Galen's anatomy. Wrong. Lecture 2.
- **c) Koch's isolation of the cholera bacillus (1883 to 1884). Correct.** Pacini had seen the organism in 1854, but Koch isolated it in pure culture.
- d) Graunt's tables. Wrong. Counting, 1662. The tables showed patterns but not causes.

**Q7 (slide 39). Semmelweis cut childbed fever deaths in Vienna in 1847 by:**

- a) quarantine of the ward. Wrong. Not what the slide describes.
- **b) handwashing in chlorinated lime. Correct.**
- c) a new vaccine. Wrong. No vaccine was involved.
- d) opening the windows. Wrong. The intervention was hand hygiene.

**Q8 (slide 40). Florence Nightingale is also remembered in public health for:**

- a) discovering the cholera bacillus. Wrong. Pacini and Koch (slide 25).
- **b) the polar-area diagram showing deaths by cause. Correct.** Slide 28.
- c) the first smallpox vaccine. Wrong. Jenner, 1796 (slide 30).
- d) the Public Health Act 1848. Wrong. That was the Chadwick era (slide 11).

# 9. Common misconceptions and myths

| Myth | Accurate version |
|---|---|
| "Removing the pump handle ended the 1854 outbreak." | Attacks fell from 143 on 1 September to 12 on 8 September, the day the handle came off. They were already falling. Many residents had fled. Snow himself wrote it was "impossible to decide" whether the well was still dangerous. The handle was a sensible precaution and a symbol, not proof. |
| "Snow found the cause by drawing a map." | His hypothesis came first (1849). The map came later and supported it. Others had used maps to argue for miasma. A map shows. Comparison proves. |
| "Snow is the father of epidemiology." | The label is contested. In the 1850s Farr was the recognised authority and the official Board of Health rejected Snow's water theory. Safer wording: "a founder of modern epidemiological method". |
| "Snow beat miasma overnight." | No. Farr's elevation data fitted miasma, and the General Board of Health inquiry still rejected Snow in 1854. Farr changed his mind after the 1866 East London outbreak. Slow change is normal in science. |
| "Chadwick's table shows life expectancy." | It shows average age at death. That figure depends on the age mix of the town. Farr pointed this out almost at once. |
| "Chadwick was right." | His action (water, drains) was right. His theory (miasma) was wrong. |
| "Farr's elevation law was just wrong." | The data were right. Low ground also got the dirtiest water. The explanation was wrong. |
| "Semmelweis was ignored because he was wrong." | His result was right, but there was no mechanism yet. The slide gives the lesson: a right result with no mechanism is easy to dismiss. The percentages are [verify]. |
| "Nightingale's nursing alone cut deaths at Scutari." | The slide credits a Sanitary Commission that fixed drains and ventilation. Her figures are her own and are debated. |
| "Bhore invented rural health care in India." | Rural medical schemes existed before Bhore. The Committee set out an integrated plan (1943 to 1946). The slide says "too simple". |
| "India's last smallpox case was in 1977." | May 1975 (Assam). India was certified free in April 1977. WHO declared eradication in 1980. |
