# Slide-by-slide notes

## Opening (slides 1 to 5, 5 minutes)

**Say**

- This is the first of three lectures. The thread: explanation by tradition and belief (today), then by observation and experiment, then action on whole populations.
- Read the four objectives aloud (slide 3).
- The coloured part of the timeline on slide 4 is today: prehistory, the first river civilisations, and the classical world.

**Ask** (slide 5, the toe): "Who made this toe, and why? Was it for walking or for looks?" Expected answer: there is no single answer. See "The Question slides".

**Show/Do.** Slide 2 shows the three-lecture arc. Slide 5 shows the Greville Chester toe.

**Watch out.** The slide 5 image licence is "to be confirmed". Do not share the deck outside class until that is checked.

## Prehistory and farming (slides 6 to 11, 5 minutes)

**Say**

- Fire gave cooked food, warmth, protection and a gathering place. It also kills some germs and parasites in food and water. Injured and disabled people survived in some early groups (slide 6).
- Slide 8 shows two skulls with holes cut in them (trepanation, an old skull operation). Some skulls show bone regrowth at the edges. So the person lived on after the operation.
- Settled life brought crowding, animals living close to people, shared water, stored grain and waste (slide 9).
- In the southern Levant, farmers' skeletons show more infection lesions than hunter-gatherers' (205 vs 200 skeletons compared). A study in the German loess zone found no sign of epidemics (slide 10).
- The safe summary: farming tended to raise exposure to infection, but the picture is complex.

**Ask.** "What does the way people live decide?" Expected answer: which diseases they get (slide 10 callout).

**Show/Do.** Slide 7 is two pictures of stone tools. Name them (Old Stone Age and New Stone Age) and move on in 30 seconds. Slide 11 shows three cradles: Fertile Crescent, India, China.

**Watch out**

- The claim on slide 6 that disabled people survived has no source on the slide. The research notes say a citation is still needed. Say "in some early groups" and do not name a site.
- Do not say farming "made people sicker". The deck wording is "tended to raise exposure".
- Slide 11 says the cradles gave "safe-to-drink water". The notes do not check this. Treat it as general background.

## Egypt and Mesopotamia (slides 12 to 17, 6 minutes)

**Say**

- Ebers (about 1550 BC) is a scroll of about 110 pages, with roughly 700 remedies and spells. Edwin Smith (about 1600 BC) holds 48 trauma cases (slide 12).
- In Ebers, remedies give the ingredients and how to use them. Many come with a spell. Medicine and religion were not separate (slide 13).
- Edwin Smith cases follow a pattern: title, examination ("If you examine a man having..."), diagnosis, treatment (only if treatable), gloss (slide 14). Show the pattern on the slide, then click for the callout.
- The three verdicts are "an ailment I can treat", "an ailment I will contend with" and "an ailment not to be treated". Prognosis means a forecast of how the illness will go.
- Hammurabi's laws (about 1750 BC, Babylon): laws 215 to 223 deal with the surgeon. Fees depended on the patient's class. A large incision that cured earned 10 shekels from a free man and 5 from a freed man. If the patient died, the surgeon's hands could be cut off (slide 16).

**Ask.** (Slide 14) "Why would a doctor write 'an ailment not to be treated'?" Expected answer: honest limits, and it protects the doctor. Link to prognosis. (Slide 17) "What do payment rules and penalties for doctors remind you of today?" Expected answer: fees, malpractice and liability.

**Show/Do.** Slide 15 (Tomb of Nakht) is background only: grain, beer and the Nile. Give it 20 seconds.

**Watch out**

- Edwin Smith has no separate "prognosis" heading. The verdict does that job. Do not say Imhotep wrote it. The notes call that speculation.
- Do not call Edwin Smith "the first mention of the brain". The notes say to check this first.
- Slide 17 calls Hammurabi "the first written rules" for doctors. The notes do not check the word "first". Say "early written rules".
- Slide 17 mentions informed consent. The laws say nothing about consent. Present it as a loose comparison.
- The laws speak only of the surgeon with the knife. Herb healers and incantation healers are not in them.

## Indus Valley (slides 18 to 21, 5 minutes)

**Say**

- Mature Harappan period: about 2600 to 1900 BC. Show the Great Bath and the brick water channel (slide 18).
- Brick-lined drains ran from houses to covered street drains with inspection holes. There were private bathrooms, terracotta pipes and soak pits. Mohenjo-daro had more than 700 wells (slide 19).
- Read the warning callout aloud: we know the engineering. We do not know it reduced disease. The purpose of the Great Bath is also uncertain.

**Ask** (slide 21): "Which modern public health programme does Indus sanitation remind you of?" Expected answer: safe water and sanitation: Swachh Bharat, Jal Jeevan Mission, WASH.

**Show/Do.** Slide 20 is a picture of social structure. Name it and move on.

**Watch out.** Students will say the drains show the Indus people "knew about germs". Correct this: the slide title says "sanitation before germ theory". The evidence is the structures. The health effect is inferred.

## Vedic India, Sushruta, Arthashastra (slides 22 to 28, 6 minutes)

**Say**

- Health and disease are explained in the Vedas with prayers, herbs and water. Ayurveda grew from this tradition. Health is balance: the three doshas (vata, pitta, kapha). Two great texts: Charaka (medicine) and Sushruta (surgery) (slide 22).
- Sushruta's date is disputed. Traditionally "about 600 BC"; the text grew over several centuries. The oldest dated manuscript is AD 878. Traditionally quoted: 184 chapters, 1,120 conditions, about 300 procedures, over 120 instruments (slide 23).
- Cutting off the nose was a punishment, so reconstruction was needed. The text describes a cheek-flap repair, using a leaf as a template (slide 25).
- The pupil's charge is given at the start of training. Teachers, friends, the poor and the helpless are treated without payment (slide 26).
- Arthashastra: attributed to Kautilya, about 300 BC; assembled between roughly 150 BC and AD 300. Book 4, chapter 3: against pestilence, use physicians with medicines and ascetics with purificatory rites (slide 27).

**Ask.** (Slide 26) "The same passage excludes some people from treatment. Is that acceptable?" Use it as a short ethics discussion. The notes name the excluded: a professional hunter, a fowler, a habitual sinner.

**Show/Do.** Slides 24 and 28 are pictures only (manuscript pages; map of the Mauryan empire). Use 20 seconds each.

**Watch out**

- Do not say "Sushruta invented the forehead flap". The text describes a cheek flap. The forehead flap is documented later and reached Europe through a 1794 report.
- Do not say Sushruta lived "a millennium before Hippocrates". The notes warn against this.
- The counts on slide 23 are traditional figures. Say "traditionally quoted".
- Slide 23 says the text covers "anatomy by dissection". The notes do not check this line.
- The notes say do not put sanitation rules or hospitals in the Arthashastra on any slide until checked in Olivelle 2013.
- The doshas are listed under Ayurveda. Do not tell students the Vedic hymns themselves list the three doshas unless you have checked.

## Greece (slides 29 to 32, 5 minutes)

**Say**

- Hippocrates was born about 460 BC on Kos. The Corpus has many authors over centuries. Epilepsy is "nowise more divine nor more sacred than other diseases". Disease has natural causes, so it can be studied (slide 29).
- *Airs, Waters, Places*: begin with the seasons, winds and waters of a place. People who drink marsh water have "large and obstructed spleens". This is early environmental epidemiology, and a clue to malaria (slide 30).
- The humours: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile. Health is balance, as in the doshas. Treatment: diet, rest, bleeding (slide 31).
- Oath: "I will do no harm or injustice to them"; "I will keep secret... all such things"; "I will not use the knife, even upon those suffering from stones" (slide 32).

**Ask.** "What changed in the Hippocratic approach?" Expected answer: illness has natural causes, so it can be studied; the environment matters.

**Show/Do.** Slide 31 shows the four temperaments diagram. Slide 32 shows a Byzantine copy of the oath arranged as a cross.

**Watch out**

- The title "father of medicine" is not on any slide. Explain it as a later label. The first biography is much later, so personal details are late and legendary.
- Say "Hippocratic writers", not "Hippocrates wrote". The Corpus is multi-author.
- The humoral scheme in *Nature of Man* is usually credited to Polybus. The notes mark this as not verified.
- *Airs, Waters, Places* is not strictly a humoral text.

## Beyond Greece (slides 33 to 35, 3 minutes)

**Say**

- China: *Huangdi Neijing*, compiled in the Han period, about 2nd to 1st century BC. The Yellow Emperor attribution is traditional. Health is balance: yin and yang, five phases and *qi* (flow of vital energy) in channels. It values prevention (slide 33).
- Alexandria: Herophilus (about 335 to 280 BC) and Erasistratus (about 304 to 250 BC) dissected human bodies. Herophilus placed intelligence in the brain and timed the pulse (slide 34).
- Rome: the Cloaca Maxima, the Aqua Appia (312 BC), Frontinus lists nine aqueducts by about AD 97, and Vitruvius advised building away from marshes (slide 35).

**Ask.** (Slide 35) "How does Rome compare with the Indus Valley?" Expected answer: both built water and drain systems without germ theory; Rome ran them as state works.

**Show/Do.** Slide 33 has two columns. Read the callout: prevention before illness.

**Watch out**

- Slide 33 says "two thousand years before screening and vaccination programmes". The notes do not check this line. Treat it as a rhetorical point. Variolation (deliberate inoculation against smallpox) is recorded in China only from about the 16th century.
- Slide 34: the claim that prisoners were cut open alive is disputed. Say so.
- Slide 34 says human dissection was "rare in the ancient world". The notes do not source this.

# Optional activity (5 minutes)

**Pair and match.** Run it after slide 21 or after slide 35.

1. Write this on the board: "One ancient practice. One modern public health programme."
2. Give pairs 2 minutes. Each pair picks one practice from today (for example Indus drains, Rome's aqueducts, the Arthashastra's state response to pestilence, Chinese prevention) and names a modern match.
3. Take 4 or 5 pairs. Write each as "ancient / modern" in two columns on the board.
4. For each pair, ask: "Do we know the ancient practice improved health, or do we only know it was built?" This reuses the warning on slide 19.

**Output.** Photograph the board. Open Lecture 2 with one line from it. This covers objective 4.

**Time.** If you run it, you use the slide 21 time plus 3 minutes. Take the 3 minutes from the quiz: ask only Q1, Q2, Q4, Q5 and Q7.

# Quiz

**How to run it (about 8 minutes).**

- Option A: hands up. Read each question aloud. Give 15 seconds. Ask for a show of hands for a, b, c, d. Say the answer and the one-line reason from this guide. Do not show slide 45 until the end.
- Option B: phone poll. Type the question and options into a poll tool. Show the result bar. Then give the reason.
- Keep to about 55 seconds per question. If time runs out, stop at Q6 and read out the answers to Q7 and Q8.

## Answers and reasons

**Q1. Covered drains and private bathrooms in towns. Answer: b) Indus Valley.**
a) Egyptian: the Egypt slides are about papyri and farming. They show no town drains. c) Sumerian: the Mesopotamia slide is about Hammurabi's laws on surgeons. d) Greek: the Greek slides are about natural causes and the environment.

**Q2. The Edwin Smith papyrus. Answer: c) a set of trauma cases with exam, diagnosis and a verdict.**
a) A spell book: this is the trap. Ebers mixes remedies and spells. Edwin Smith is a set of cases. b) A list of kings: wrong. d) A map of the Nile: wrong.

**Q3. Sushruta is chiefly associated with: b) surgery.**
a) Physiology: not what the Sushruta slides cover. c) Epidemiology: closer to *Airs, Waters, Places* and the Arthashastra. d) Pharmacology: closer to the Ebers remedies.

**Q4. *Airs, Waters, Places* argued that: b) the environment influences disease.**
a) Gods send disease: this is the view the writer of *On the Sacred Disease* argues against. c) Blood circulates: later, in Lecture 2. d) Germs cause fever: germ theory comes much later.

**Q5. Moving from hunting to farming most likely: b) tended to raise exposure to infection.**
a) Removed infections: the opposite. c) Had no effect: the evidence points to a rise, though not everywhere. d) Ended injuries: nothing on the slides says this. Note the careful words "most likely" and "tended".

**Q6. Text attributed to the Mauryan minister Kautilya: c) Arthashastra.**
a) Rig Veda: the Vedas appear on slide 22 as health ideas, not state duties. b) Charaka Samhita: a medicine text. d) Manusmriti: not covered in this lecture.

**Q7. Aqueducts and a great sewer, built without germ theory: a) Rome.**
b) Egypt: papyri. c) Sumer: Hammurabi's laws. d) Han China: the *Huangdi Neijing* and prevention. The Indus Valley also built drains. The question names aqueducts and a great sewer, so only Rome fits.

**Q8. The *Huangdi Neijing* is notable for: d) health as balance, with stress on prevention.**
a) The first surgery text: the surgery texts in this lecture are Edwin Smith and Sushruta. b) A list of kings: wrong. c) The first microscope study: that is Leeuwenhoek, Lecture 2.

# Common misconceptions

| Myth | Accurate version |
|---|---|
| Ancient medicine was all magic | Edwin Smith has an examination, a verdict and treatment. Ebers mixes drug, ritual and observation. Both are on slides 13 and 14 |
| Farming made people sicker | Farming tended to raise exposure to infection. The picture is complex (slide 10) |
| Sushruta invented the forehead nose flap | The text describes a cheek flap. The forehead flap is later (slide 25) |
| Sushruta lived 600 BC, a millennium before Hippocrates | The date is disputed. "Traditionally about 600 BC" (slide 23) |
| Hippocrates wrote all the Corpus and invented the humours | The Corpus has many authors. The humoral scheme is usually credited to Polybus (not verified) |
| The Indus drains proved the people prevented disease | We know the engineering. We do not know the health effect |
| The Arthashastra was written by Chanakya about 300 BC | It is attributed to Kautilya. It was assembled between about 150 BC and AD 300 |
