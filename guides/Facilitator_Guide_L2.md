# Slide-by-slide notes

## Block 1: Open and hook (slides 1 to 5, 6 minutes)

**Say**

- "Lecture 1 ended with Greece: disease has natural causes, and the environment matters."
- "For about 1,500 years after that, one book-based tradition (Galen, the humours) set the rules." Today's question is how that hold was broken.
- The four tools are dissection, experiment, the microscope and counting (slide 3).
- The coloured phase on the timeline is about AD 500 to 1700 (slide 4).

**Ask.** Slide 5 (the question slide, see the section below).

**Show/Do.** Slide 5 shows the 1543 title page of the *Fabrica*. Point at Vesalius at the table himself.

**Watch out.** Slide 2 says "about 1,500 years". Objective 1 says "over 1,000 years". The two numbers differ. Say "more than a thousand years" and do not defend a single figure.

## Block 2: Medieval period (slides 6 to 10, 8 minutes)

**Say**

- Galen was born in Pergamon in AD 129. His death is given as about AD 200 or 216. His anatomy relied largely on animal dissection. For centuries a Galenic text was the final answer (slide 6).
- Al-Razi lived about 865 to 925. He separated smallpox from measles by symptoms. About 40 Latin editions of his smallpox treatise appeared between 1498 and 1866 (slide 7).
- Ibn Sina (980 to 1037). The *Canon of Medicine* was completed around 1025. About 15 Latin editions were printed in the last 30 years of the 1400s. It was used in teaching into the 1600s (slide 8).
- The Al-'Adudi hospital in Baghdad opened in AD 981. A ruler's endowment (*waqf*) paid for it, not the patient. It was destroyed in 1258 (slide 9).
- Black Death: Messina, October 1347. Venice 1348: 40 days. Ragusa 1377: 30 days for ships, 40 days for land travellers. *Quaranta* means forty (slide 10).

**Ask**

- Slide 9: "How is this different from a district hospital today?" Expect: funding, who it serves, a lasting institution.
- Slide 10: "Did they need to know the cause to act?" Expect: no. (The slide gives the answer after a pause.)

**Show/Do.** On slide 10, wait for the pause before the callout. Let students answer first.

**Watch out**

- Slide 6: the "reading Galen aloud while someone else dissected" line has no source in the research file. Say "in many schools".
- Slide 7: the figure of 40 editions is cited to Hajar, but it comes from another source.
- Slide 9: the aside contains a note meant for you (the Al-Razi site story cannot be true). Do not read it out.
- Slide 10: "half of Europe" is popular but contested. Say "up to a third or more, varying by region". "First quarantine" depends on the definition (Venice 1348 or Ragusa 1377).

## Block 3: Renaissance anatomy (slides 11 to 15, 7 minutes)

**Say**

- Da Vinci dissected human bodies and drew what he saw. The habit that matters is look, then record (slide 11).
- Vesalius: *De humani corporis fabrica*, Basel, June 1543, aged 28. He corrected about 300 of Galen's errors using human dissection. The mandible is one bone, not two. Men and women have the same number of ribs. The great vessels do not start in the liver. He kept Galen's idea that veins and arteries carry different kinds of blood (slide 12).
- Printing let the same accurate picture reach every school (slide 13).
- Paré, 1537, Italian campaign: the boiling oil for gunshot wounds ran out. He used a salve of egg white, rose oil and turpentine. The salve group did better. Ligature after amputation is recorded from 1552 and is older than Paré. His motto: "I dressed him, God healed him" (slide 14).
- The Paré story looks like a trial: two groups, different treatment, compare outcomes (slide 15).

**Ask**

- Slide 12: "If Vesalius corrected Galen, why is he still partly wrong?" Expect: he inherited the veins and arteries idea. The method is the revolution, not the man.
- Slide 14: "Is this a proper trial?" Expect: no. It was unplanned, and he told the story himself.

**Show/Do.** On slide 13 ask students to compare the two plates. Both show careful, accurate drawing.

**Watch out**

- Do not say Paré invented ligature, or that ligature replaced boiling oil. The salve replaced the oil. Ligature is older.
- Do not tell the "Church persecuted Vesalius over a missing rib" story. The slide says it is not well supported.
- Slide 11: the scan source of the da Vinci image is still to be confirmed.

## Block 4: Paracelsus, Fracastoro, Harvey (slides 16 to 19, 6 minutes)

**Say**

- Paracelsus died in 1541. He used chemicals and minerals against humoral treatment. The quote on the slide is from his *Seven Defences* (written 1538, printed 1564) (slide 16).
- Fracastoro (1478 to 1553). His poem of 1530 gave syphilis its name. *De contagione* (1546): disease spreads by "seeds" through contact, objects, or at a distance. This is about 300 years before germ theory (slide 17).
- Harvey, *De Motu Cordis*, Frankfurt, 1628. Tie the arm: the veins swell below the band, and the valves allow blood to go one way only (slide 18).
- The arithmetic: more than one thousand heartbeats in half an hour. Even the smallest guess gives more blood than the body holds. So blood goes round (slide 19).

**Ask**

- Slide 17: "If Fracastoro was right, why did miasma still win?" Expect: no way to see the seeds, so no proof.
- Slide 19: "What did Harvey not see?" Expect: capillaries. Malpighi saw them after Harvey's death.

**Show/Do.** Slide 19: work the arithmetic on the board with one assumed value, for example half an ounce per beat. Let a student multiply.

**Watch out**

- Slide 16: the Latin slogan is a later summary. Quote the German text as translated on the slide.
- Slide 17: the sources on the slide were not opened in the research file. Check them first.
- Slide 19: the slide says "per hour" but the quoted passage is "half an hour". Use the words "half an hour" when you speak. Do not use the popular "540 lb an hour" figure.

## Block 5: Leeuwenhoek (slides 20 to 22, 4 minutes)

**Say**

- A cloth merchant in Delft with no university training. He made his own single-lens microscopes. Surviving lenses reach about 270 times magnification (slide 20).
- Letter to Henry Oldenburg, Royal Society, 9 October 1676: five kinds of "animalcules" in pepper water, rain, well, sea and river water. An excerpt was printed in 1677 (slide 20).
- Slide 21 shows his own drawings.
- The Royal Society could not at first reproduce the pepper-water result. One source says Hooke showed members "little animals" in November 1678. The point is replication (slide 22).

**Ask.** Slide 22: "Why did the Royal Society doubt a draper?" Expect: no training, no one else could see it.

**Show/Do.** Slide 21: ask what a doctor of 1676 would have made of "little animals" in water.

**Watch out.** Do not say Leeuwenhoek invented the microscope. The deck says he made his own lenses. The 1678 detail rests on one source.

## Block 6: Graunt and counting (slides 23 to 26, 6 minutes)

**Say**

- London draper, 1620 to 1674. One book, 1662, on the Bills of Mortality. Presented to the Royal Society on 5 February 1662. Elected a Fellow within weeks (slide 23).
- Slide 24 shows a real Bill: 15 to 22 August 1665, a plague year.
- He used the Bills to estimate London's population, give a careful male-to-female ratio at birth, show that city deaths exceeded city births, compare London with the countryside, separate epidemic from endemic causes, and estimate infant mortality (slide 25).
- Limits: his survival estimate was rough, not a true life table. Halley's 1693 Breslau table is usually called the first proper one. Petty may have helped with the book (slide 26).

**Ask**

- Slide 25: "What can counting show that no single physician can see?" Expect: a pattern across a population.
- Slide 26: "Ask of any statistic: who counted, what, and how?" Ask students to apply it to a number they met this week.

**Show/Do.** Slide 24: let students read the Bill for ten seconds. Ask what a column of numbers could do that one case could not.

**Watch out.** The "14 boys to 13 girls" figure is only in secondary sources. Do not quote it. No true portrait of Graunt exists, so do not search for one.

## Block 7: Bridge and recap (slides 27 and 28, 2 minutes)

**Say**

- Bentham, 1776: the measure of right and wrong is "the greatest happiness of the greatest number". The phrase is older than Bentham.
- If suffering matters, sanitation and poor-law reform become duties. This is the idea behind Lecture 3.
- Recap (slide 28): medieval authority; Renaissance looking again; experiment; microscope and counting.

**Watch out.** Slide 27 carries a callout telling you not to claim that Chadwick was Bentham's secretary. This is a note to the teacher, not to students. The research file says the claim is not verified. The gap facts file and the newer Beamer deck say it is true. Do not say it until you have checked.

# The question slide

There is one slide headed "Question" (slide 5). Other slides ask a question inside a callout (slides 9 and 10).

**Slide 5.** "Why did it take until 1543 to correct Galen?"

Expected answers:

- Galen's books were the test of truth.
- Human dissection was rare, and Galen's anatomy relied largely on animals.
- Printing had not yet spread accurate pictures.
- Few people dared to check a revered authority.

To draw students out:

- "What would you have needed to dare to contradict Galen?"
- "Who would have lost out if Galen were wrong?"

If the room is silent: ask students to turn to a neighbour for 30 seconds, then take one answer per pair. If someone gives a wrong answer, such as "nobody had thought of it", ask "what would stop a clever person checking?" Then move on. Do not spend more than three minutes. The answers appear in the lecture itself.

# Optional 5-minute activity: "What did they do that a book-only doctor would not?"

**Use it in place of the recap on slide 28.** This raises the total to 50 minutes.

1. Think (1 minute). Each student picks one of Vesalius, Paré, Harvey, Leeuwenhoek or Graunt. They write one sentence: what did this person do that a doctor who only read books would not do?
2. Pair (2 minutes). Partners compare. Do they use the same tool?
3. Share (2 minutes). Take five answers.

**Output.** Draw a table on the board with four columns: dissection, comparison or experiment, the microscope, counting. Place each name under its tool as students call out answers. (Vesalius under dissection; Paré under comparison; Harvey under experiment and arithmetic; Leeuwenhoek under the microscope; Graunt under counting.) Leave the table on the board. It answers objective 4 and is your recap.

# Quiz

**How to run it (6 minutes).** Use hands up for a, b, c, d, or a phone poll. Show one question, give 20 seconds, take a show of hands, then reveal the answer on slide 36 after the last question. Ask one student per question to give a reason. If you want longer discussion, drop the activity.

|---|---|---|
| 1. Whose anatomy did Vesalius correct? | b) Galen | a) Hippocrates: the humours belong to him, but the *Fabrica* corrects Galen's anatomy. c) Harvey: he came later (1628). d) Paracelsus: he attacked humoral treatment with chemicals, not anatomy |
| 2. William Harvey is known for | b) circulation shown by experiment and measurement | a) the microscope is Leeuwenhoek's. c) capillaries: Malpighi saw them after Harvey's death. d) vaccination is Lecture 3 |
| 3. Paré is remembered for popularising | b) ligature of arteries | a) boiling oil is what he moved away from (1537, he used a salve). c) opium only: not in the deck. d) amputating less: not in the deck. Ligature is older than Paré, which is why the stem says "popularising" |
| 4. "Only the dose determines..." is attributed to | c) Paracelsus | a) Vesalius is anatomy. b) Paré is surgery ("I dressed him, God healed him"). d) Graunt is counting |
| 5. John Graunt is important because he | c) analysed death records to find patterns in a population | a) the first microscope: Leeuwenhoek made his own lenses. b) cholera's source: Snow, in Lecture 3. d) an anatomy atlas: the *Fabrica* plates |
| 6. The first quarantine measures show that | b) populations can be protected without knowing the cause | a) is the opposite. c) quarantine failed: the deck makes no such claim. d) germ theory came later, and the plague bacterium was found in the 1890s |
| 7. Fracastoro's *De contagione* proposed spread by | c) "seeds" passed by contact, objects, or at a distance | a) divine anger is the older supernatural theory. b) humours are the Hippocratic and Galenic idea. d) marsh air only: his three routes include contact and objects |

# Common misconceptions

| Myth | Accurate version |
|---|---|
| Galen was careless or foolish | His anatomy came largely from animals. That is the source of the errors |
| The Church persecuted Vesalius over a missing rib | Not well supported (slide 13) |
| Paré invented ligature, or ligature replaced boiling oil | A salve replaced the oil in 1537. Ligature is older and is recorded from 1552 |
| Paracelsus said "sola dosis facit venenum" | The Latin is a later summary. The German is on slide 16 |
| Harvey saw capillaries, or "540 lb an hour" | Malpighi saw capillaries later. The 540 lb figure is not from the text |
| The Black Death killed half of Europe | "Up to a third or more", varying by region |
| Quarantine needs germ theory | The first quarantines (1348, 1377) came 500 years before the plague bacterium was found |
| Leeuwenhoek invented the microscope | He made his own single-lens microscopes |
| Graunt invented the life table | His estimate was rough. Halley's 1693 table is usually called the first proper one |
| Al-Razi chose the site of the Baghdad hospital | He died about 56 years before it opened |
