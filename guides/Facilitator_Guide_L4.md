# 1. At a glance

**Aim.** Show students that medicine became more powerful in the twentieth century, yet health still follows the conditions of life, and that research must be done ethically.

**Audience.** Third-year MBBS students, Community Medicine.

**Length.** 45 to 50 minutes (plan 49). It can be split into two sessions (see section 4).

**Room and kit.** Projector, a browser for the HTML deck, a clicker or keyboard. Sound is not needed. Have the PDF as a back-up.

**Files.**

- `HoM_L4.qmd` (source), `HoM_L4.html` (the deck to teach from), `HoM_L4_typst.pdf` (PDF back-up).
- The deck has 35 slides: slide 1 is the title, then 34 slides under `##` headings. Eight of them are quiz slides.
- Lectures 1 to 3 come first. Lecture 3 ends at about 1900. Lecture 4 starts there.

**Prep time.** About 2 hours. Spend most of it on the fact check in section 2.

# 5. Slide-by-slide notes

### Block 1: Opening (slides 2 to 4)

**Say**

- Lecture 3 ended with counting, mapping and sanitation making whole populations the unit of care. Germ theory explained why it worked.
- Today we cover what the twentieth century added: better drugs, global institutions, and a harder question. Why are some people healthier than others?
- The thread today: killing germs, organising care, changing the conditions of life, and doing it ethically.
- Read the five objectives (slide 3) quickly.

**Ask.** "What was the main method of public health in Lecture 3?" Expected answer: count, compare, act.

**Show/Do.** Slide 4 shows the timeline. The three bullets give the eras: 1900 to 1950, 1950 to 1990, 1990 to now.

**Watch out.** Slide 4 uses the image `timeline_L3.svg`. That image highlights the Lecture 3 period, not the Lecture 4 period. There is no L4 version of the timeline. Point to the last phase on the right and say "we are at the end of the line today". Or tell students the colour band is for the previous lecture.

### Block 3: Flexner, antibiotics, epidemiology (slides 6 to 8)

**Say**

- Flexner, 1910: the Carnegie Foundation asked Abraham Flexner to review medical schools in the United States and Canada (Bulletin No. 4). Result: laboratory science, hospital teaching, university links. Many weak schools closed.
- The gain was better training and safer practice. The cost, said critics, was a narrow focus on the individual patient and disease, and the closure of many Black and women's colleges.
- Antibiotics: Fleming noticed the mould in 1928. Florey, Chain and colleagues made it a drug. Nobel Prize 1945. First treated patient: Oxford, 1941. Resistance followed within decades.
- Epidemiology: Framingham (1948). Doll and Hill: case-control study of smoking and lung cancer (1950), British doctors cohort (1954). Bradford Hill's viewpoints for judging cause (1965).

**Ask.** "Is a drug a population tool or a patient tool?" Expected answer: a patient tool first. Population control needs delivery, surveillance and many people reached.

**Show/Do.** On slide 6, read the callout: Community Medicine exists partly to put back what the biomedical model left out. On slide 8, tell students this is the toolkit from the epidemiology module.

**Watch out**

- Slide 6 cites Hiatt and Stockton 2003 in the aside. That source is not in the reference list on slides 34 and 35. Check it before you quote it.
- Slide 7 gives sulfonamides 1935 and streptomycin 1943 as "the first effective TB treatment". These dates are not in the source notes. Check them.
- Slide 8 says Framingham "names" risk factors. This is loose wording. The aside tells you to check Dawber 1951.

### Block 4: WHO and NHS, transition, Lalonde (slides 9 to 11)

**Say**

- WHO constitution came into force 7 April 1948. The definition of health: "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."
- The NHS began 5 July 1948, based on the Beveridge Report (1942). Care free at the point of use, paid by tax.
- Omran (1971) described three stages: pestilence and famine; receding pandemics; degenerative and man-made diseases. India has both burdens at once.
- Lalonde (Canada, 1974): four influences on health. Human biology, environment, lifestyle, health care organisation.

**Ask.** "Which of the four Lalonde parts gets most health spending?" Expected answer: health care organisation (the slide says so). Then ask: "Which parts cause much of the burden?" The other three.

**Show/Do.** On slide 9, contrast two models: a global agency that sets norms, and a national system that funds care.

**Watch out**

- Slide 10 says "India now carries both burdens at once." This is not sourced on the slide. Offer a local example (TB and hypertension), or check a source first.
- Slide 11 says most health spending went to the fourth part while much of the burden came from the other three. This claim is not in the source notes and the aside only cites Lalonde 1974. Check it in the report before you say it as fact.

### Block 5: Alma-Ata, Ottawa, determinants (slides 12 to 15)

**Say**

- Alma-Ata, 6 to 12 September 1978: primary health care, community participation, action across sectors. Goal: Health for All by 2000.
- In 1979 Walsh and Warren proposed selective primary health care (a few cheap interventions, such as GOBI). Many saw this as a step back. The Astana Declaration of 2018 renewed the commitment.
- Ottawa Charter, November 1986: build healthy public policy, create supportive environments, strengthen community action, develop personal skills, reorient health services.
- Determinants: Virchow (1848, Upper Silesia), Black Report (1980), Whitehall studies, WHO Commission (2008): *Closing the gap in a generation*. Gaps are shaped by power, money and resources, so they are not natural.

**Ask.** "Which Ottawa action area would Chadwick have liked best?" Expected answer: build healthy public policy (the slide says: compare Chadwick, policy first).

**Show/Do.** Slide 12 has the Alma-Ata photograph. Slide 15 is the second Question (section 6).

**Watch out**

- Slide 12 says "USSR. WHO and UNICEF conference" and explains GOBI by name only. Neither is in the source notes. Check them. Lecture 3 gives the Declaration date as 12 September 1978; slide 12 gives the conference span. Make both consistent when you speak.
- Slide 14: Virchow's "medicine is a social science" is a later paraphrase (the aside says so). Say "credited with".
- Slide 14 lists Marmot 1978 in the aside. Check the year and journal before you cite it.

### Block 6: Research ethics (slide 16)

**Say**

- Nuremberg Code (1947): from the Doctors' Trial. First principle: voluntary consent is essential.
- Tuskegee (1932 to 1972): men with syphilis were studied. It stopped after press exposure.
- Declaration of Helsinki (1964, World Medical Association, revised many times). Belmont Report (1979): respect for persons, beneficence, justice.
- India: ICMR guidelines, first issued 1980, latest 2017.

**Ask.** "In each case, what came first, the harm or the code?" Expected answer: the harm. The codes were written after.

**Show/Do.** Read the callout: this is the doctor's old duty (Sushruta, Hippocrates), now written as rules for research.

**Watch out**

- Teach this slowly. Nuremberg and Tuskegee involve real people who were harmed. Name the harm plainly, avoid gruesome detail, and do not use jokes. Say that the victims were people, not "subjects".
- Say clearly that Tuskegee followed Black men with syphilis from 1932 to 1972, and that it stopped only after press exposure. Check Jones 1981 before you say more (for example about consent). Invite students to think about who has less power in research today.
- The slide says the Public Health Service "withheld proven treatment". The source notes only say the men were untreated. Check Jones 1981 before you say "proven".
- Allow a short pause after this slide. Some students may have a personal link to these stories.

### Block 7: India (slides 17 and 18)

**Say**

- 1896: plague in Bombay. 1897: Haffkine's plague vaccine and the Epidemic Diseases Act. The same Act was used again in 2020.
- 1946 Bhore Committee (Lecture 3). 1952: Community Development Programme and the first primary health centres. 2005: National Rural Health Mission. 2018: Ayushman Bharat (Health and Wellness Centres plus PM-JAY).
- Polio: EPI 1978, UIP 1985, Pulse Polio from 1995. Last wild case 13 January 2011. WHO certified South-East Asia polio-free on 27 March 2014.
- Pattern: simple tools, delivered at scale, with good data.

**Ask.** "What did polio elimination need besides a vaccine?" Expected answer: mass campaigns, surveillance of acute flaccid paralysis, and workers going house to house.

**Show/Do.** Slide 18's callout links polio with smallpox (Lecture 3).

**Watch out.** The aside on slide 17 says "Check dates before citing". Do so. EPI, UIP and Pulse Polio dates are not in the source notes.

### Block 8: COVID-19, AMR, One Health, data and AI (slides 19 to 22)

**Say**

- COVID-19: WHO declared a public health emergency of international concern on 30 January 2020 and a pandemic on 11 March 2020. WHO estimated about 14.9 million excess deaths in 2020 and 2021. Vaccines were authorised in about a year.
- Old tools came back: surveillance, testing, isolation, quarantine, masks, the 1897 Act.
- AMR: the GRAM study (Lancet, 2022) estimated about 1.27 million deaths directly caused by bacterial resistance in 2019 and about 4.95 million associated. Link to Lister and Semmelweis: infection control is a front line again.
- One Health: human, animal and environmental health. Quadripartite alliance of FAO, WOAH, UNEP and WHO (2022).
- Data and AI: new tools, old questions. What does it show, and what does it not?

**Ask.** "Which tool from Lecture 3 ran the COVID dashboards?" Expected answer: Snow and Farr's methods (counting, mapping, comparing).

**Show/Do.** Slide 22 is optional. Use it only if you are on time.

**Watch out**

- COVID figures change. Check the latest WHO numbers, and say the date you checked. The slide's own aside says to do this.
- Slide 19 says "a first for mRNA vaccines". This is not in the source notes. Check it.
- Slide 21 says "Most new infections of people come from animals". This is not in the source notes. Check it, or say "many".
- Slide 21 says the origin of COVID-19 is "still debated". Keep to that wording and do not go further.

### Block 9: Recap and take-home (slides 23 and 33)

**Say**

- Flexner, penicillin, epidemiology: science-based care, then risk-factor thinking.
- WHO, Alma-Ata, Ottawa: health as a right, built on primary care and promotion.
- Omran, Lalonde, determinants: disease follows the conditions of life.
- Ethics: consent and justice, written after harm.
- Take-home: health still follows the conditions of life. Ask of any new tool: who gets it, who is left out, and what did they consent to?

# 6. The Question slides

### Slide 5: medicines or living conditions?

- **Prompt.** "Which has saved more lives since 1900: medicines, or clean water, food, vaccines and better living conditions?"
- **Method.** Ask for a show of hands for each side. Then press the next key to show the callout.
- **Expected answer.** Much of the fall in infectious death rates came before effective drugs. The slide calls this McKeown's argument and says it is still debated.
- **Silent room.** Ask a concrete question: "Name one infection you think is controlled mostly by a drug. Name one controlled mostly by water or vaccines." Or ask students to talk to the person next to them for 30 seconds.
- **Watch out.** McKeown is named on the slide, but there is no McKeown reference on slides 34 and 35. Say clearly that it is an argument, not a proven fact. Do not give figures.

### Slide 15: two women, same illness

- **Prompt.** "Two women in the same district have the same illness. One recovers, one dies. List three reasons that have nothing to do with the drug."
- **Expected answers.** Income, distance to a facility, education, caste, gender, health insurance, nutrition, work conditions. These are the determinants.
- **Silent room.** Start with one example yourself, for instance distance to a facility. Ask the class to add two more. Write the list on the board.
- **Watch out.** Do not name real patients. Keep caste and gender examples factual and respectful.

# 7. Optional 5-minute activity: map a programme

**Task.** Pick one local programme, for example immunisation or TB. Map it onto Lalonde's four parts and the five Ottawa action areas.

**Steps**

1. Put students in pairs (1 minute).
2. Each pair draws two small lists: Lalonde (human biology, environment, lifestyle, health care organisation) and Ottawa (policy, supportive environments, community action, personal skills, reorienting services).
3. Pairs write one real action from the programme under each heading (3 minutes).
4. Take two or three pairs to share (1 minute).

**What to do with the output.** Find the empty boxes. Ask: "Which box has nothing in it for this programme? That is a gap." Put the list on the board and keep a photo. Use it at the start of the next session.

# 8. Quiz

**How to run it (6 to 8 minutes).**

- Show one question at a time (slides 24 to 31). Give 30 seconds each.
- Ask for hands or answers by cards (a, b, c, d). Do not give answers until slide 32.
- Show slide 32 (answers) and spend a short time on any item where many hands were wrong.
- If you are short of time, use items 1, 4, 5 and 7.

**Items**

| Item | Correct | Why the others are wrong |
|---|---|---|
| 1. 1910 report that made US medical education science-based | b) Flexner | Bhore (1946) is an Indian health report. Black (1980) is the UK report on social class and health. Lalonde (1974) is the Canadian health field report. |
| 2. WHO definition of health | c) complete physical, mental and social well-being | "Absence of disease" is what the definition says is not enough. "A good diet" and "long life" are not in it. |
| 3. Ottawa Charter is mainly about | b) health promotion | It is not about vaccination schedules, surgical training or drug approval. |
| 4. Epidemiologic transition | b) from infection and famine toward degenerative and man-made diseases | Option a is the reverse. Option c and option d describe other shifts (place of care, rural to urban), not Omran's idea. |
| 5. First principle of Nuremberg Code | c) voluntary consent | Ethics committee review, payment and randomisation are not the first principle of the code. |
| 6. Alma-Ata called for | b) primary health care as the route to Health for All | The conference was not about vaccination only, privatising hospitals, or vertical programmes. Slide 12 says the selective approach was a later, narrower proposal. |
| 7. Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 was first used for plague and used again in | d) 2020 | 2005, 2009 and 2014 are not the date the slide gives. Slide 17 says the same Act was used in 2020 for COVID-19. |
| 8. Best description of AMR | c) a rising threat linked to antibiotic use in people, animals and the environment | It is not only for hospitals, not solved, and not only in rich countries (the slides link resistance to people, animals and farms). |

### Quiz notes

- **Item 1** has two ideas in one stem ("made science-based" and "is criticised"). Suggested: "Which 1910 report made US medical education science-based? a) Bhore b) Flexner c) Black d) Lalonde". Then ask the criticism as a separate discussion.
- **Item 4** is weak. The right answer (option b) is the longest option, so students can pick it by length. Option d is a different idea (urbanisation). Suggested: "Omran's epidemiologic transition describes a shift in the main causes of death from: a) chronic diseases to infections b) infections and famine to chronic and man-made diseases c) young adults to children d) none of these". Keep all options about the same length.
- **Item 7** says "first used for plague". The source notes only say the Act dates from 1897, and that the plague link is "to verify". The distractor years have no basis in the slides. Suggested: "The Epidemic Diseases Act, passed in 1897, was invoked again in which year? a) 1947 b) 1975 c) 2005 d) 2020". Check the facts first.
- **Item 6** uses the word "vertical programmes" which was never explained on the slides. Say it aloud ("programmes for one disease only") or change it to "programmes for one disease only".
- **Answer key skew.** Option b is correct for items 1, 3, 4 and 6 (four of eight). Shuffle the options before you use this as a test.

# 9. Common misconceptions and myths

- **"Flexner was all good" or "Flexner was all bad".** Both are wrong. The slide gives gains (better training, safer practice) and costs (narrow focus, closed Black and women's colleges). Let students weigh both.
- **The WHO definition of health.** It is famous and often criticised. The slides do not discuss criticism. If a student raises one (for example that "complete" well-being is hard to measure), welcome it and say it is a fair point. Do not invent details.
- **Alma-Ata and selective primary health care.** Students think they are the same. They are not. Alma-Ata was broad (community participation, action across sectors). Walsh and Warren (1979) proposed a narrower package (GOBI), which many saw as a step back.
- **"Medicine saved us."** Slide 5 raises McKeown's argument: much of the fall in infectious deaths came before effective drugs. The slide says it is still debated. Present it as an argument, not a settled fact.
- **Tuskegee details.** Keep to what the slide says: 1932 to 1972, Black men with syphilis, stopped after press exposure. Check Jones 1981 for further facts. Do not add details from memory.
- **COVID counts.** The reported death count and the WHO excess-death estimate (about 14.9 million for 2020 and 2021) are different measures. Say which one you are quoting, and give the date.
- **"Public health is only about infections."** The epidemiologic transition and the determinants slides show it also covers chronic disease and social conditions.

# 11. Closing paragraph for the whole series

Say this at the end of the last lecture:

"Across four lectures we moved from explanation by authority, to explanation by observation, to action on populations, to the conditions that make people healthy. The method is the same each time: count, compare, act, and check. Chadwick counted. Farr compared. Snow acted. Nightingale checked, and she showed it in a picture. Today's tools are faster, but the questions are old: what does the data show, who is left out, and did they agree?"

*Note.* The names in this paragraph are from Lecture 3 (Nightingale is a slide added to Lecture 3). If your class did not see Lecture 3, shorten it.
